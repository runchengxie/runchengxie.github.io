Today, let's look at a case that helps explain the limits of Buffett's early investing: Marshall-Wells.

## 0. In One Sentence

Marshall-Wells showed that Graham-style investing at low valuations could protect against downside risk, but that was only the first step in investing.

The second step, earning a return, often requires an additional catalyst, such as a competitive advantage, capital allocation, improvements to the business model, or a change in control.

## 1. Why Buffett Bought It

Buffett made this investment while he was still studying at Columbia.

To attend the Marshall-Wells shareholder meeting, he even asked his teacher David Dodd whether he could miss a class. He went only after Dodd agreed.

Marshall-Wells was extremely cheap at the time:

1. Its share price was below net current asset value.
2. Its `EV/EBIT` was very low.
3. Cash, securities, and inventory made up a high proportion of its assets.
4. Liabilities and preferred stock did not impose much of a burden.
5. On paper, it looked like a classic Graham-style cigar-butt stock.

Its inventory consisted mainly of hardware, tools, and household goods that did not spoil and faced relatively little risk of obsolescence, so the discount to book assets was not particularly alarming.

The company's past operating record was also very good: over many years before and after the war, revenue and profit had generally grown, and it had remained consistently profitable.

## 2. Why He Soon Sold

After attending the shareholder meeting, Buffett quickly realized that financial statements alone could not give him a complete understanding of a company.

At the meeting, he found management's attitude extremely perfunctory, as if it were merely completing a legal formality, with little sense of accountability to shareholders. This deeply irritated the young Buffett.

He also later found other opportunities, so he soon exited the investment, earning almost nothing on paper and coming close to a small loss.

## 3. Reviewing the Investment

In the real world, companies often decline quietly. Even Buffett did not recognize it quickly at the time, and still believed the company would soon rebound. Marshall-Wells's situation was roughly as follows:

1. Postwar shortages temporarily benefited traditional hardware wholesalers.
2. As the market returned to normal, profit margins fell.
3. Competition began to intensify.
4. Specialized wholesalers, retailer cooperatives, and chain retailers had lower-cost, more flexible business models.
5. The old-fashioned wholesale structure of companies like Marshall-Wells was too cumbersome, and they gradually lost their advantages.

Buffett did not get involved in managing the company at the time. He was still a student, and even if he had wanted to intervene, doing so would have been difficult. Simply expecting an undervalued stock to return quickly to fair value was therefore unrealistic.
