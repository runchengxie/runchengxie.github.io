Today, let's look at one of Buffett's early cigar-butt investments: Dempster.

## 1. In One Sentence

Dempster was an ordinary business with declining core operations but fairly substantial assets on its books. To Buffett, companies like this could deteriorate only so fast, while their assets provided a cushion against losses.

## 2. What Was Going On at the Company?

Dempster originally made windmills, water pumps, and irrigation equipment. By the 1960s, the windmill industry was in decline because rural electrification had made electric pumps more convenient. But the company did not collapse immediately, for two reasons:

1. Old equipment generated service and parts revenue: after a windmill was sold, it continued to bring in revenue from spare parts and repairs, so the decline was gradual.
2. It began selling other farm machinery, such as seeders and fertilizer equipment. Competition was fiercer, but these at least gave it some room to develop new business.

## 3. Why Buffett Bought It

The key was valuation.

According to its 1961 balance sheet, the company had book net assets of about $4.6 million, or $76.48 per share. Buffett applied steep discounts to these figures:

1. Receivables were valued at 85%.
2. Inventory was valued at 60%.
3. Liabilities were counted at 100%.

Even under this conservative calculation, he put the company's quick realizable value at roughly $35 per share. His average purchase price was about $28 per share, which meant:

1. A discount of approximately 63% to book net assets.
2. A price approximately 20% below his own conservative valuation.

In the terminology of his teacher Graham, this extreme discount made it a net-net: a stock trading below its net current asset value per share.

## 4. What Buffett Did

He made this a control investment. By the end of 1961, Buffett Partnership directly owned 70%, with affiliated parties indirectly holding another 10%, effectively giving him control of the company.

He then found that the existing management had limited operating ability and that there was considerable room for basic improvements:

1. Spare parts and complete machines were not priced differently.
2. Inventory management was poor.
3. Cost control was poor.
4. Unprofitable branches were not closed promptly.

Buffett later brought in Harry Bottle to take over management. Bottle took several key steps:

1. Converted inventory into cash as quickly as possible.
2. Sharply cut selling, general, and administrative (SG&A) expenses.
3. Closed loss-making locations.
4. Disposed of old inventory.
5. Used the released funds to repay debt and buy securities.

Buffett bought at about $28 per share. By his exit in 1963, the share price had risen to $80, producing an excellent return.

The turnaround consumed considerable effort and further prompted Buffett to consider whether he should continue with the cigar-butt approach to investing.
