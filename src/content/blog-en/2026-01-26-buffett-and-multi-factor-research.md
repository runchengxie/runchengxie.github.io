title: "Paper Review: Where Buffett and Multifactor Quantitative Research Converge"
date: 2026-01-26

---

Several classic studies in finance examine multiple factors. In plain language, they ask a common question: what characteristics do stocks with higher long-term returns tend to share?

Many of their findings echo Buffett's experience. Here are four of the most representative points of overlap.

## 1. Novy-Marx's Finding: Both Cheap and Good

One of Novy-Marx's central findings is that companies with high gross margins and strong profitability deliver higher average returns even at relatively higher valuations. Controlling for profitability also substantially improves the performance of value strategies.

In his 1979 shareholder letter, Buffett noted that a poor business bought cheaply is inferior to a good business bought at a reasonable price. In 1982, he added that even a marvelous business can become a terrible buy if its purchase price only appears fair.

## 2. The Risks of Indiscriminate Expansion

CMA, the investment factor in the Fama-French five-factor model, indicates that companies investing more aggressively have lower average returns after controlling for other factors. The paper also identifies a particularly poor-performing group: small companies with high investment and low profitability, which it calls lethal portfolios.

Berkshire is a diversified holding company, and Buffett has repeatedly emphasized that capital allocation should focus on capital efficiency rather than simply pursuing scale. In his 2014 shareholder letter, for example, he used the LTV conglomerate as a cautionary example.

## 3. Frequent Trading Costs Significantly Drag on Performance

Carhart's results are straightforward: expense ratios, turnover, and sales charges, or loads, all have significant associations with poorer risk-adjusted performance. The regression coefficients suggest that investors receive essentially no corresponding benefit for the costs they bear.

In his regressions, the expense-ratio coefficient is approximately -1.54, and the turnover coefficient approximately -0.95. Interpreted on an annualized basis, every 100bp increase in expenses or turnover creates a substantial drag on alpha.

In his 1983 shareholder letter, Buffett called excessive market activity a pickpocket of business. He also worked through the arithmetic: if shares turn over 100% in a year, with a 1% commission charged on both purchases and sales, shareholders pay 2% of the company's net worth each year for that transfer. That consumes one-sixth of a company's 12% ROE. It is an expensive game of musical chairs and a form of self-imposed taxation.

## 4. The Main Disagreement: Short-Term Investment Opportunities

One major difference between Buffett and these quantitative studies concerns short-term momentum. Jegadeesh and Titman's research shows that buying past winners and selling past losers produces significantly positive returns over holding periods of 3 to 12 months. The most successful 12-month/3-month strategy earns approximately 1.31% per month, with results affected by whether the most recent week is skipped.

Buffett does not trade for the short term. Asked how to predict an individual stock's performance over the next year, he would probably give his classic answer: I have no idea how stocks will perform over a year.
