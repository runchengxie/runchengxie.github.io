Researchers find that quantitative funds still struggle to fully replicate Buffett.

## 1. Quantitative Researchers Have Already Tried Systematic Replication

A paper by Hou, Mo, Xue, and Zhang points out that a quantitative strategy selecting only stocks with low valuations cannot fully represent Buffett's investment approach.

Starting from the security analysis framework used by Buffett and his teachers Graham and Dodd, the researchers attempt to express this approach as a more formal theory of asset pricing.

## 2. The Researchers' Theoretical Foundation

Their underlying theory is called the investment CAPM, or investment-based asset pricing.

The name sounds complicated, but the intuition is simple: stock prices must ultimately connect to a company's investment decisions, returns on investment, and the return required by the market.

This echoes Buffett's famous idea: when you buy a stock, you are buying the business behind it.

They identify three core dimensions of pricing that should sound familiar to anyone who knows Buffett:

1. Companies with strong profitability are generally worth more.
2. But if their share prices are still low, those companies are cheap.
3. Good companies bought cheaply often offer higher expected returns.

The third point is precisely the proposition the paper seeks to formalize. Across stocks, those with high expected returns tend to combine better quality with modest valuations. This logic does not require assuming market inefficiency first. The researchers argue that it can coexist with an efficient-market framework.

Why can good, cheap companies exist in a nearly efficient market? Because the market leaves investors who carefully analyze financial fundamentals some room to earn returns, though that room is limited and difficult to exploit.

## 3. Buffett Is Constantly Imitated but Remains Hard to Surpass

After analyzing more than half a century of US stock data and the historical performance of thousands of actively managed funds, the researchers find that their model still cannot fully explain Buffett's excess returns.

Specifically, from February 1968 through December 2020, Berkshire's monthly excess return averaged 1.41%, exceeding the return explained by their q-factor model by 0.59%.

Another surprising result concerns expected growth, which the researchers had assumed Buffett would value and included in the q-factor model. In the Berkshire sample, its loading has the opposite sign.

The researchers argue that their model's inability to fully fit Buffett's performance reflects the complex economic meaning embedded in many financial statement items. Quantitative models struggle to match the depth of Buffett-style judgment. For example, research and development, advertising and brand effects, employee training, management quality, long-term capital allocation ability, and organizational capabilities all affect future profitability, yet standardized factors struggle to capture them fully.

The researchers' replication effort achieved partial success. The q / q5 models capture the quantifiable parts of Buffett's investment logic with statistical significance. But investment managers such as Buffett still exercise some judgment that standard factor models struggle to absorb and imitate.

## 4. References

Hou, Mo, Xue, Zhang, *The Economics of Security Analysis* (Management Science, 2024), and its predecessor, *The Economics of Value Investing* (NBER, 2017)
