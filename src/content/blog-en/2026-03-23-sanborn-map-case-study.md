Sanborn Map's securities were worth more than the company's market capitalization. Buffett bought shares and helped arrange an exchange through which shareholders received part of those assets, linking realization of the discount to shareholder action.

## Source dates and applicability

The case concerns the late 1950s and 1960. Its main source is the partnership letter issued January 30, 1961 about 1960; the 2005 Q&A is a later recollection. As of October 2026, the case illustrates asset discounts and shareholder action. Historical tax arrangements and market conditions require separate assessment, and the 50% remark is no present return promise.

## 1. The Map Business and Its Securities Assets

Sanborn Map was originally a good business with a strong monopoly position. Later, its main customers, insurance companies, switched to using individual building risk cards for underwriting; revenue from its map business fell sharply, and it gradually became a declining industry.

Buffett discovered that the securities portfolio on its books was worth more than the market capitalization of the entire company, while its core business still had some earning power. He therefore increased his stake and sought support from other shareholders to unlock that value.

## 2. Why Buffett Bought It

Sanborn originally produced fire insurance maps. Insurers relied on these extraordinarily detailed maps to assess buildings' fire risks, including building materials, windows, water pipes, surrounding structures, and more. The business had several powerful characteristics:

1. High upfront investment, but low subsequent maintenance costs.
2. Once a city had been mapped, it was difficult for newcomers to enter.
3. Customers needed standardization, making a single nationwide mapping system valuable.
4. Long-term revenue came from subscriptions and revision fees, providing a high-quality, sustainable income stream.

This was a classic early information monopoly: invest capital to build a database, then profit from economies of scale and barriers to entry. In today's terms, you could think of it as a specialized data services company.

## 3. The Layers of Value Buffett Saw

The first layer: the core business had deteriorated, but it was not beyond repair.

1. Demand for traditional insurance maps had declined, but still existed.
2. Its maps and surveying capabilities could also serve many customers outside insurance.
3. In 1958–1959, the map business remained profitable after tax, but annual after-tax profits had fallen below $100,000.
4. The company as a whole remained profitable and also earned investment income.

The second layer: the real value was on the balance sheet.

Sanborn's total market capitalization at the time was about $4.73 million, but the basket of stocks and bonds it held had a market value of about $7 million.

## 4. Buffett Personally Drove Changes to Unlock Value

Cigar-butt investments often require active investor involvement; waiting for the market to correct itself is usually not enough. This work is tedious and demanding, which was also one reason Buffett gradually reduced this type of investing later on.

Over two to three years, Buffett carried out the following steps:

1. Continued buying shares and sought support from other shareholders.
2. Pushed to separate the operating business from the investment assets.
3. Enabled shareholders to realize the portfolio's value through a securities exchange.
4. Optimized the tax structure to reduce the drag from capital gains taxes.
5. Left capital in the map business to give it a chance to improve operations.

This process was already very close to what would later be called activist investing: taking an active role in realizing a company's value.
