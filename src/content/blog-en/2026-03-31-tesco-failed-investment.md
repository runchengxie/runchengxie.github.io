Today we look at Tesco, a case that helps explain how Buffett increased his losses by selling too slowly.

## 0. In One Sentence

Because he took too long to sell, an investment that could still have produced a profit ultimately became a failure with a cumulative after-tax loss of approximately $444 million.

## 1. What the Company Looked Like at the Time

Tesco is a British retail giant whose origins go back to 1919, when Jack Cohen founded it. In the company's own description, it is a leading multinational food retailer operating in five markets: the United Kingdom, Ireland, Slovakia, the Czech Republic, and Hungary.

In publicly available material, Buffett did not set out his investment rationale as fully as he did for Coca-Cola, so we cannot know his complete thinking at the time.

But his public comments suggest a fairly straightforward starting point: this was a large, strongly positioned supermarket leader whose operations appeared stable and predictable.

By the end of 2012, Berkshire held 415 million Tesco shares at a cost of about $2.3 billion. In his 2014 shareholder letter, Buffett also said Tesco was the leading food retailer in the United Kingdom both then and at the time of writing.

## 2. Signs of Deterioration Had Appeared, but He Still Sold Too Slowly

In January 2012, Berkshire increased its Tesco stake from 3.21% to 5.08%. Even though Tesco had already issued a profit warning, Buffett still considered it worth adding to, or at least did not judge its problems to be structural deterioration. The percentages still need checking against the original ownership-change notice.

The real problems began accumulating before the accounting scandal came to light.

By July 2014, Kantar data showed that Tesco's U.K. market share had fallen from 30.7% when Phil Clarke took over in March 2011 to 28.9% in June 2014. Over the same period, Aldi's share rose from 2.1% to 4.7%, and Lidl's from 2.5% to 3.6%. Cheaper competitors were steadily eating into Tesco's core business. The individual figures still require verification against Kantar’s original series; the shareholder letter below supports the general decline in market share.

For a low-margin supermarket business that depends on turnover and scale, even a small loss of market share can have a much uglier effect on the income statement.

Buffett did notice the problems; he simply acted too slowly.

According to his own account in the 2014 shareholder letter, he had become somewhat disenchanted with the management in 2013 and sold 114 million shares for a $43 million profit, but retained most of the position.

He later called this behavior `dawdling`. Munger was less forgiving, calling this kind of behavior `thumb-sucking`.

## 3. The Accounting Problems Quickly Turned the Loss into Reality

In 2014, the situation began deteriorating more rapidly.

Buffett wrote in his letter that Tesco's problems worsened month by month, with falling market share, shrinking margins, and emerging accounting problems.

In September 2014, Tesco publicly acknowledged that its expected first-half profit had been overstated by £250 million, mainly because income from commercial agreements with suppliers had been recognized prematurely. The figure was subsequently revised upward to £263 million, including approximately £145 million of income from earlier years.

In December 2014, Tesco issued its fourth profit warning in five months, saying group trading profit for the 2014–15 financial year would not exceed £1.4 billion. That was far below analysts' expectations of approximately £1.94 billion and less than half the £3.8 billion earned in 2011–12. The company announcement directly confirms the £1.4 billion guidance; the warning count, analyst consensus and historical comparison still require their contemporary records.

By this stage, it was difficult to explain the sharp fall in the share price as a short-term market misjudgment.

After Berkshire sold all its remaining shares during 2014, the investment ended with an after-tax loss of approximately $444 million. In October of that year, Buffett publicly acknowledged that investing in Tesco had been a huge mistake.
