When Buffett's purchase of a stock becomes public, does the market immediately drive its price higher?

Hughes, Liu, and Zhang's 2010 paper *Overconfidence, Under-Reaction, and Warren Buffett's Investments* answers no. More surprisingly, some professional investors even trade in the opposite direction.

## 1. What the Paper Studies

The authors examine Berkshire's 13F holdings disclosures, covering roughly 1980 through 2006, and do two main things:

1. Construct copycat portfolios: simulate following the disclosed holdings to see whether doing so is profitable.
2. Conduct event studies: examine the market's immediate response to disclosure and its behavior over the following months.

## 2. The Finding: Remarkably Slow Market Reactions

Stock prices do rise a little at disclosure, but the adjustment is far from complete.

More importantly, prices continue to drift after disclosure. Even investors who follow with a delay may see prices keep rising.

The market does not immediately absorb all the information in Buffett's purchases. That information may instead be reflected in prices gradually over the following period.

## 3. A More Striking Pattern: How Professionals Respond

The paper documents a notable pattern: when disclosures show that Berkshire has increased its stakes in certain stocks, some professional investors move in the opposite direction:

1. Institutional ownership actually declines, suggesting selling.
2. Analyst recommendations deteriorate, suggesting downgrades or a more pessimistic outlook.

The authors suggest that this may relate to overconfidence and to underestimating the importance of public information. Many professionals put greater faith in their own judgment, leading them to react slowly to changes in Buffett's holdings or even trade against them.

Consequently, the information continues to be incorporated into prices gradually after disclosure.
